Understanding where the edge actually exists

Polymarket doesn’t reward prediction in these short crypto windows.

It rewards execution priority.

Every order sits in a queue, and fills are distributed strictly based on who arrived first.

Imagine two identical bids at 50c.

Same size, same price.

The system doesn’t care who is smarter or who has better analysis.

It will always fill the order that was placed earlier.

That means your entire edge reduces to one variable: how early your bot interacts with the order book.

What most people miss is that the order book is not empty before the event starts.

It opens far earlier, and early participants quietly accumulate queue position.

By the time volume arrives, they are already sitting at the front, collecting fills while late entrants watch trades pass through without execution.

Detecting market creation before others

To win this game, your bot cannot rely on manual discovery.

You need to subscribe directly to the data layer of Polymarket.

In practice, that means polling or subscribing to their API endpoints where new markets are listed.

A simple loop that checks every few seconds is already too slow if you’re competing against optimized systems.

You want a websocket or event-driven listener that reacts the moment a new market ID appears.

python

import requests

import time

seen = set()

while True:

    markets = requests.get("https://api.polymarket.com/markets").json()

        if m["id"] not in seen:

            seen.add(m["id"])

            handle_new_market(m)

    time.sleep(1)

This is where most bots fail, because they treat order placement as a single action instead of a burst.

The critical detail is minimizing delay between order creation and submission.

Even small inefficiencies in signing or RPC communication can push you behind competitors.

In practice, this means:

> pre-loading wallet state

> keeping nonce management ready

> using fast RPC endpoints

Your goal is to reduce the time between "market detected" and “orders live” to the lowest possible value.

Designing the ladder for maximum fill probability

Placing orders early is not enough.

You also need them at the right levels.

If you spread your liquidity randomly, you’ll sit in the queue but never get filled.

The trick is to place orders where price is likely to interact during the window.

This usually means clustering orders around expected volatility zones rather than distributing them evenly.

For example, if BTC tends to move within a certain range during 5-minute windows, your bot should bias order placement toward those probabilities.

Mathematically, this becomes a distribution problem.

Instead of equal sizing, you apply a weighted function.

This ensures that when volume arrives, your orders are not just early, but also relevant.

Every time you cancel and replace an order, you go to the back of the line.

That means naive update loops destroy your edge.

A well-designed bot minimizes unnecessary modifications and only adjusts when the market structure has clearly shifted.

This requires state tracking.

Your bot needs to know:

> which orders are still active

> where they sit relative to the spread

> whether adjustment is worth losing priority

Holding an early position is often more valuable than chasing a slightly better price later.

That feedback loop is slow and often expensive.

READ THIS BEFORE BUILDING A TRADING BOT Your orderbook is fast but you're measuring the wrong thing. And it's why your fills still fail... Here's what real HFT guys measure that you don't: They never brag that their orderbook is fast. They measure tick-to-trade. That's the full path, from the packet arriving to your decision leaving: > Packet in > Decode > Update the book > Snapshot the top of book > Run your logic > Encode > Packet out You probably optimized one stage of that and never timed the rest. The slow part is almost never where you think. It's decode, snapshot, signing and serialization. One trick worth stealing from the real HFT world. Stop re-sorting the entire book every tick just to find your best levels. Keep a maintained cache of the top 5 bids and asks, and update it only when something changes. Sorting the full book on every snapshot is a silent tax you pay on every single tick. Now the part that matters for Polymarket specifically: Real HFT shops win by rewriting in C++ because on a real exchange that pipeline IS the bottleneck. On Polymarket it is not. Take this wallet as example: <https://polymarket.com/@ritb123?via=punisher> The CLOB sits behind a ~20ms delay you cannot remove. Your shiny rust rewrite saves maybe 0.5ms. The 20ms swallows it whole. So measure your tick-to-trade first. Find your actual slow stage. Fix that one. Don't rewrite the whole thing in rust to win a race the CLOB already lost for you.
